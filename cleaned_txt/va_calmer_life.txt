About This Workbook
This workbook is a self-help guide for managing worry, anxiety, and stress. It teaches practical coping skills for people who feel tense, restless, overfocused on possible problems, or stuck in repeated worry.

The workbook explains that worry and stress often overlap. Worry can involve repeated thoughts that something bad may happen, while stress includes the body’s threat response and the strain that follows from stressful events or stressful thinking.

What Worry Looks Like
Worry often means thinking repeatedly about possible future problems, such as health, finances, family, work, or safety. People who worry a lot often find these thoughts hard to control, and the worry can interfere with sleep, concentration, decisions, and daily life.

The workbook notes that worry can affect mood, physical health, memory, pain, and quality of life. It can also lead to more medical use, more disability, and more strain over time.

When Worry Becomes a Problem
Worry and stress are part of normal life, and sometimes a little worry can help people prepare or solve a problem. It becomes more of a problem when it happens too often, feels too intense, lasts too long, is hard to stop, or gets in the way of life goals and daily functioning.

The workbook encourages thinking about both the benefits and the costs of changing old worry habits. Staying the same can feel familiar and easier in the short term, but it may keep worry affecting health, relationships, and well-being in the long term.

Two Steps to Reducing Worry
The workbook describes two main steps for reducing worry and stress. The first is becoming more aware of situations, symptoms, and patterns connected to worry. The second is learning and practicing coping skills to use when worry or stress begins to build.

These skills are meant to become a personal toolbox. A person may use the same tool often or choose different tools depending on the situation and the type of symptoms showing up at that moment.

Becoming Aware of Worry and Stress
Worry and stress show up in three main ways: physical symptoms, thoughts, and behaviors. Becoming aware of these patterns makes it easier to notice worry early and respond before it grows.

Physical symptoms can include muscle tension, fast pulse, shortness of breath, sweating, trembling, and butterflies in the stomach. Thoughts usually involve feared outcomes, negative predictions, or repeated concerns. Behaviors include actions taken to reduce distress, such as avoiding something, checking too much, putting things off, or using distractions in an unhealthy way.

Separating Thoughts, Feelings, and Body Symptoms
The workbook emphasizes that body sensations, thoughts, and feelings are not the same thing. A physical symptom is a body sensation like tightness in the chest, a thought is an idea or prediction, and a feeling is an emotion such as fear, sadness, or anger.

These often happen together. For example, a worrying thought may lead to anxious feelings and body symptoms like a racing heart or tense muscles.

Daily Awareness Practice
A useful awareness exercise is to record one stress-related situation each day. The situation does not have to be severe; even a small stressful moment can help build awareness.

A simple daily reflection includes:
What situation triggered worry or stress
Where you were and who was involved
What body symptoms you noticed
What thoughts were running through your mind
What you avoided because of worry
What you did too much to try to reduce worry

Practicing this regularly helps identify your own worry style and makes it easier to choose a coping tool sooner.

Deep Breathing
One of the first calming skills in the workbook is deep breathing. Worry often leads to quick, shallow breathing, which can increase dizziness, lightheadedness, and feelings of anxiety.

Deep breathing helps relax the body and can also slow racing thoughts. The workbook describes it as teaching the body to be calm.

How to Practice Deep Breathing
The workbook teaches two main features of deep breathing. First, breathe slowly, evenly, and deeply. Second, breathe from the diaphragm rather than the upper chest.

A basic deep breathing sequence is:
Inhale through the nose to a count of 4
Exhale through the mouth to a count of 4
Do not pause after each inhale
Place one hand on the stomach and one on the chest
Let the stomach hand move more than the chest hand
Keep the breathing slow and even

The workbook recommends regular daily practice for about 10 to 15 minutes and also using the skill during real-life worry situations.

Optional Spiritual Support
The workbook includes optional religious or spiritual support for people who find it comforting. During breathing or coping practice, a person may choose to focus on a meaningful word, image, prayer, or spiritual phrase.

This is presented as optional and not necessary for the skills to work. People who do not want to use spiritual content can skip it.

Calming Thoughts
Calming thoughts are short statements said to oneself to reduce worry and stress. They are meant to guide the mind in a steadier direction when distress begins to rise.

Examples include:
I can do what I need to do
If I take it one step at a time, I can handle this
Even if I make mistakes, it will be okay
It is not the worst thing in the world
My worry will not hurt me

How to Use Calming Thoughts
A calming thought works best when it feels believable and personally meaningful. The workbook suggests thinking about what you would say to a friend in the same situation and then offering similar words to yourself.

It also recommends saying calming thoughts out loud when possible, writing them on a note card, placing them somewhere visible, or recording them to replay later. Repeating them often makes them easier to remember in stressful moments.

Depression and Worry Together
The workbook notes that worry and depression often occur together. When people feel down or experience loss or life disruption, they may stop doing activities they once enjoyed or that gave them meaning.

This can create a cycle in which low mood leads to less activity, and less activity then worsens mood. Increasing pleasant or meaningful activities can help interrupt this cycle and support mood improvement.

Rating Activities and Mood
One way to understand mood patterns is to look at daily activities and rate how each one affected mood. The workbook uses a 1 to 5 scale, from very bad to very good, and encourages thinking about why a particular activity affected mood in a certain way.

This helps identify activities that improve mood, create satisfaction, or increase loneliness, guilt, or sadness. It also helps people notice that not all activities affect them in the same way.

Pleasant and Meaningful Activities
The workbook encourages adding pleasant, satisfying, or meaningful activities back into life. These can include social contact, outings, physical activity, self-care, hobbies, acts of kindness, volunteering, or spiritual practices if personally meaningful.

The goal is not to become busy for its own sake. It is to reconnect with activities that support enjoyment, purpose, accomplishment, or connection.

Planning Small Activities
When restarting activities, the workbook recommends choosing one or two that are not too hard and can be broken into small steps. A plan works better when it includes the activity, the steps needed, and a target completion date.

It also encourages asking whether the activity actually helped mood after it was completed. This helps build evidence about what supports well-being.

Behavior Patterns That Maintain Worry
The workbook highlights common behavior patterns that keep worry going. These include putting things off, avoiding people or places, repetitive behaviors, over-checking, and other habits used to lower distress in the short term.

These behaviors may feel helpful briefly because they reduce immediate discomfort. Over time, however, they usually keep the person from learning they can cope, and they may make the original problem worse.

Avoidance and Procrastination
Avoidance can include delaying bills, appointments, conversations, decisions, or social situations because they feel stressful. Procrastination reduces distress for the moment, but the problem often remains and may grow larger.

The workbook suggests that one way to reduce this pattern is to gradually face what is being avoided while using calming skills at the same time.

Repetitive Behaviors and Checking
Some people respond to worry by doing something too much, such as smoking, snacking, pacing, cleaning, reading the same material repeatedly, or repeatedly checking whether something is okay.

These actions can become a way of managing distress without actually solving the problem. The workbook recommends noticing when such behaviors are unhealthy, excessive, or keeping you from doing what matters.

Facing Worry Instead of Feeding It
A key message in the workbook is that long-term improvement often requires facing worry rather than always escaping it. This can mean opening the mail, making the appointment, speaking up once, or checking something once instead of many times.

At first this may increase worry. With practice, the distress often becomes easier to manage, especially when paired with tools like breathing or calming thoughts.

SMART Goal Setting
To change worry-related behavior, the workbook recommends setting SMART goals. Goals should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and timed.

A practical goal identifies exactly what behavior will change, how success will be measured, whether the goal is realistic, why it matters, and when it will be done. If a goal is too difficult, it should be broken into smaller steps.

Behavior Change Plan
A useful behavior-change plan includes:
The worry-related behavior you want to change
One to three small steps
A completion date
The calming skills or support you will use
A note about whether you completed the plan fully, partly, or not at all

The workbook also notes that repeated practice may be needed before the situation feels easier. Early attempts may still feel stressful.

Problem Solving
Problem solving is included for situations where worry blocks clear thinking. Instead of only spinning in circles mentally, the workbook teaches a more direct way to approach a problem.

The problem-solving structure can be summarized as:
Select a specific problem
Open your mind to all possible solutions
List the pluses and minuses of each option
Verify the best solution
Enact the plan
Decide whether it worked

This helps turn vague worry into a clearer action process.

Progressive Muscle Relaxation
Progressive muscle relaxation helps reduce the physical side of worry and stress. The workbook notes that tension often builds gradually, especially in the neck and shoulders, and people may not notice it until they are already uncomfortable or in pain.

The method involves tensing and then relaxing different muscle groups in sequence. This helps people learn the difference between what tension feels like and what relaxation feels like.

How to Practice Progressive Muscle Relaxation
The workbook explains that the tensing part should never cause pain. If a body area already has chronic pain, the tensing part for that area should be skipped and only the relaxing part should be done.

The sequence includes these muscle groups:
Right arm
Left arm
Face
Neck and throat
Torso
Right leg
Left leg

The purpose is to recognize early signs of tension and begin relaxing sooner, before stress builds further.

Thought Stopping
Thought stopping is included for moments when the mind gets stuck looping through repeated worry thoughts. The workbook describes it as a quick skill that uses attention to the environment and present experience to interrupt repetitive thinking.

This approach is especially relevant when someone feels mentally trapped in overthinking and needs a simple way to shift attention out of the loop.

Changing Worry Thoughts
The workbook teaches that the way a person interprets a situation affects how they feel and what they do next. A negative, unrealistic thought can increase anxiety and lead to avoidance, while a more balanced thought can reduce distress and support more helpful behavior.

The first step is to identify the worry thought. The second is to evaluate how realistic it is. The third is to replace unrealistic thoughts with more realistic ones.

Common Unrealistic Thought Patterns
The workbook describes three common worry patterns:
For Sure thoughts, which assume something bad will definitely happen
Should thoughts, which use rigid rules about how people or situations must be
Big Deal thoughts, which make a difficulty feel like a catastrophe

These styles of thinking increase worry because they remove middle ground, increase pressure, or exaggerate how bad something would be.

Questions for Evaluating Thoughts
To test a worry thought, the workbook suggests asking:
Am I assuming something negative will happen
Are there other ways this could turn out
Are my expectations reasonable
Is this based on a rule about what someone should do
If this happened, would it truly be the worst thing in the world

The workbook also says some people may want to check whether a thought fits with their own religious or spiritual beliefs, though this is optional.

Replacing Worry With Realistic Thinking
The goal is not forced positivity. The workbook specifically recommends replacing worry thoughts with something more realistic, not simply something cheerful.

For example, instead of “It will definitely go badly,” a more realistic thought might be, “I do not know yet how it will go, and I can wait and see.” This kind of thought leaves room for uncertainty without assuming the worst.

Building a Calmer Life
The workbook recommends regular practice, not just using skills when distress is already high. Daily repetition helps the body and mind learn the skills more easily and makes them more available in real situations.

It encourages choosing the modules and tools that fit your own pattern of worry. Most people are encouraged to work on awareness, breathing, calming thoughts, and maintenance, then add other tools as needed.

Maintaining Progress
Maintenance means continuing to use helpful skills after the first wave of improvement. The workbook treats this as an ongoing process rather than a one-time fix.

A practical maintenance plan includes keeping awareness of symptoms, using calming tools early, continuing regular relaxation practice, noticing when old avoidance or over-checking patterns return, and reusing problem solving or thought skills when worry starts growing again.

Talking to a Doctor
The workbook advises discussing worry, stress, or depression with a doctor, especially when symptoms are persistent, interfering, or unclear. It suggests preparing by noting symptoms, how long they have been present, and what medications or remedies are being used.

This is presented as a helpful step in deciding whether additional support or treatment may be needed.

Key Support Use Cases
Help a user notice worry symptoms in body, thoughts, and behavior before they escalate

Help a user use deep breathing, calming thoughts, or PMR when feeling tense and unable to relax

Help a user interrupt worry-maintaining habits like avoidance, procrastination, or repeated checking

Safety Notes
Persistent worry and stress can affect sleep, mood, health, pain, and quality of life, so additional support may be needed when symptoms continue interfering with daily functioning

If worry, stress, or depression feels hard to manage alone, it can be helpful to discuss symptoms and current medications or remedies with a doctor
