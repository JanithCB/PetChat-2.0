About This Workbook
This workbook is a practical guide for reducing anxiety and depression through everyday coping skills. It emphasizes practice, repetition, and using skills as part of daily mental health hygiene rather than expecting symptoms to disappear immediately.

The material is designed to help people recognize symptoms, understand common patterns that maintain distress, and respond with more helpful thoughts, behaviors, and routines.

Understanding Anxiety and Depression
Anxiety and depression can affect thoughts, emotions, body sensations, and behavior. They often make people feel overwhelmed, drained, withdrawn, tense, or stuck in patterns that become hard to interrupt.

The workbook explains that some anxiety and sadness are part of normal life, but they become more concerning when they cause significant distress, interfere with functioning, or persist in ways that limit daily living.

Stress Versus Anxiety
Stress is often a response to an identifiable challenge or pressure, and it tends to fade when the stressor passes. Anxiety can continue even without a clear danger and may involve persistent fear, avoidance, panic, or repeated worry that interferes with daily life.

Examples in the workbook contrast normal nervousness before a test or public event with anxiety patterns such as panic attacks, strong social avoidance, unrealistic fears, or repetitive checking behaviors.

The Threat Response
The fight-or-flight response prepares the body to deal with danger. Once a threat is detected, the body can react automatically with faster heart rate, quicker breathing, muscle tension, sweating, tunnel vision, dry mouth, dizziness, bladder urgency, racing thoughts, trembling, and a narrowed focus on danger.

These reactions have a protective purpose, but they can feel frightening when they happen in situations that are not actually dangerous.

Common Anxiety Symptoms
Anxiety can show up physically, behaviorally, cognitively, and emotionally. Physical signs may include racing heart, chest pressure, shortness of breath, nausea, sweating, dizziness, trembling, dry mouth, and tense muscles.

Behavioral signs can include avoidance, escape, reassurance-seeking, pacing, restlessness, freezing, or difficulty speaking. Cognitive signs can include fear of losing control, fear of judgment, frightening thoughts or images, poor concentration, hypervigilance, and difficulty thinking clearly. Emotional signs often include feeling tense, afraid, edgy, frustrated, or jittery.

Common Depressive Symptoms
Depression can involve low mood, persistent sadness, fatigue, low motivation, reduced concentration, hopelessness, guilt, numbness, irritability, and loss of interest or pleasure. It can also affect sleep, appetite, energy, movement, sex drive, hygiene, and physical comfort.

Behaviorally, depression often leads to withdrawing from people, staying in bed or indoors, using substances to cope, poor self-care, crying spells, or avoiding tasks and decisions. The workbook also notes that suicidal thoughts or preoccupation with death can occur as part of depression.

Avoidance and Getting Stuck
The workbook highlights how anxiety and depression are maintained by cycles of avoidance and withdrawal. Anxiety often pushes people to escape or avoid feared situations, which brings short-term relief but strengthens fear in the long term.

Depression often pulls people toward inactivity, isolation, poor routine, and stopping things that used to support well-being. These patterns can reinforce hopelessness, low energy, and disconnection.

Square Breathing
Square breathing is presented as a simple calming tool. It emphasizes breathing from the diaphragm rather than taking shallow breaths high in the chest.

A basic square breathing sequence is:
Place one hand on the stomach and one on the chest.
Breathe in through the nose for a count of 4, letting the stomach rise more than the chest.
Hold for a count of 4.
Exhale through the mouth for a count of 4.
Pause for a count of 4 before starting again.

The workbook recommends practicing daily at a regular time, ideally in a calm setting, so the skill is easier to access when anxiety rises.

Short Relaxation Skills
The workbook includes several brief relaxation strategies for times when only a few minutes are available. These are meant to lower tension quickly and reconnect attention with the body.

Helpful short relaxation methods include:
Use diaphragmatic breathing and let the chest and shoulders relax.
Do three-part breathing by imagining the lower, middle, and upper lungs filling and emptying fully.
Gently stretch the head, shoulders, or other tense areas.
Tense and then relax muscles to feel the contrast.
Scan the body from toes to head and release tension.
Loosen the jaw by separating the teeth.
Imagine heaviness and warmth in the hands and arms.
Use a simple meditation phrase to quiet the mind.
Notice how your perceptions and attitudes shape the body’s stress response.

Breath Counting
Breath counting is a focused breathing exercise that combines attention and counting. It can help settle the mind when thoughts are racing.

A simple version is:
Sit comfortably and take a few deep breaths.
Notice the inhale, the pause, the exhale, and the next pause.
Count “one” on the inhale, “two” on the exhale, continuing up to ten.
Start over at one after ten.
If you lose count or notice the mind has wandered, gently return to one on the next inhale.

Self-Care
The workbook treats self-care as a practical mood support, not a luxury. It encourages choosing specific wellness practices, identifying roadblocks, linking the practice to personal values, and starting with the smallest realistic step.

Examples of self-care practices include pleasant activities, yoga, meditation, journaling, and other daily habits that support emotional balance. The workbook emphasizes scheduling the activity and planning how to respond if barriers arise.

Sleep Hygiene
Sleep habits are addressed as an important part of managing mood and anxiety. The workbook recommends a regular sleep-wake rhythm, even on weekends, and advises going to bed only when sleepy.

Key sleep guidelines include:
Keep bedtime and wake time fairly consistent.
If you cannot sleep after about 30 minutes, get up and do something calming until sleepy.
Avoid caffeine and nicotine for 4 to 6 hours before bed.
Avoid alcohol before bed because it can disrupt sleep quality.
Use the bed mainly for sleep and sex.
Avoid back-lit electronics for about 60 minutes before bed.
Avoid naps, or keep them under an hour and before 3 p.m.
Create a bedtime ritual such as stretching or breathing.
Avoid clock-watching during the night.
Keep the sleep space quiet and comfortable.
Try to keep daytime activities going even after a poor night of sleep.

Exercise Tips
Exercise is presented as a mood and energy support. The workbook suggests choosing enjoyable movement rather than treating exercise as punishment or extra pressure.

Helpful exercise guidance includes:
Choose an activity that feels enjoyable.
Start small, even with 10 minutes a day.
Get outside when possible.
Schedule exercise into your routine.
Change activities if boredom builds.
Use a buddy or group for accountability and connection.
Pick options that do not require expensive equipment.
Exercise when energy is best, or use movement to boost sluggishness.
Set specific, achievable goals and celebrate follow-through.

Pleasant Activities
The workbook encourages intentionally adding pleasant activities back into life. Pleasant activities can interrupt low mood, reduce emotional narrowing, and reconnect people with interest, enjoyment, and everyday reward.

Examples include listening to music, reading for fun, spending time with a pet, watching a sunset, making art, writing, shopping, nature time, games, meaningful conversation, spiritual practices, and many small sensory or social activities.

Small Behavioral Steps
The workbook emphasizes choosing do-able behaviors and taking baby steps. When mood is low or anxiety is high, small actions are more realistic and more likely to create momentum than large plans.

It suggests using small social activities, small pleasant activities, and small mastery tasks. Mastery tasks include actions such as washing a few dishes, taking out the trash, doing laundry, paying a bill, returning a message, studying one page, or organizing one small area.

Values-Based Action
Self-care and behavior change work better when connected to values. The workbook asks people to consider what values support the self-care or coping goal they want to build.

Values-based action means choosing a small behavior that reflects what matters, such as health, learning, relationships, stability, faith, creativity, or responsibility. This can make behavior change feel more meaningful and sustainable.

The Cognitive Behavioral Model
The workbook uses a CBT framework that links situation, physical sensations, emotions, thoughts, and behaviors. It shows that distress is not just one thing happening in isolation but a pattern of interacting parts.

A practical way to use this model is:
Identify the situation.
Notice the body sensations.
Name the emotions.
Write down the thoughts running through the mind.
Notice the first urge, action, or avoidance pattern.

This structure helps people slow things down and understand what is maintaining anxiety or depression in the moment.

Tracking Anxiety or Depression Patterns
The workbook encourages becoming a curious investigator of your own patterns. It asks what thoughts and behaviors maintain anxiety or depressed mood and what thoughts and behaviors help you feel okay, calm, good, or accomplished.

Examples of maintaining patterns include catastrophizing, disqualifying positives, skipping meals, staying up late, stopping exercise, avoiding friends, staying in bed, or missing responsibilities. Helpful patterns include self-credit, gentler self-talk, reading for fun, organizing a room, brushing teeth, eating breakfast, calling a friend, or going somewhere supportive.

Grounding With 5-4-3-2-1
The workbook includes a 5-4-3-2-1 grounding exercise to help return attention to the present when emotions or thoughts become overwhelming.

The sequence is:
Name 5 things you can see.
Name 4 things you can feel.
Name 3 things you can hear.
Name 2 things you can smell.
Name 1 thing you can taste or 1 good thing about yourself.

Other Grounding Skills
Grounding is described as a way to reconnect mind and body when intense anxiety or distress makes it hard to focus. Different grounding methods work for different people.

Examples include:
State your name, age, and where you are right now.
Take ten slow breaths and count them.
Splash cool water on your face.
Hold a cold or warm drink and focus on the sensation.
Hold a comforting object and notice its texture or meaning.
Listen closely to music.
If you wake distressed, orient to the room and remind yourself you are safe.
Notice clothing against the skin, feet on the floor, or your body resting on a surface.
Look around and name objects.
Walk slowly and notice each step.
Step outside and notice air, sounds, temperature, and smells.
Use grounding statements such as “This situation won’t last forever” or “These feelings will pass.”

Unhelpful Behaviors
The workbook gives many examples of behaviors that can maintain anxiety or depression. These include staying in bed, skipping meals, eating lots of junk food, avoiding friends or family, stopping exercise, missing work or school tasks, spending hours on games or TV, staying inside all the time, sleeping all day, only listening to upsetting music, or not seeking help when struggling.

The purpose of noticing these patterns is not self-judgment. It is to identify what keeps symptoms going so that more supportive alternatives can be chosen.

Unhelpful Thinking Styles
The workbook describes common cognitive distortions that can worsen anxiety and depression. These include all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralizing, mental filtering, disqualifying the positive, mind reading, fortune telling, catastrophizing, emotional reasoning, “should” statements, labeling, and personalization.

These thinking styles can make a situation feel more hopeless, threatening, or rigid than it really is. Recognizing the pattern is the first step toward responding differently.

Alternative Thoughts
When a distressing thought shows up, the workbook suggests asking whether it is fully accurate, whether there are other ways of looking at the situation, and what the bigger picture might be.

Useful questions include:
Am I looking at the whole picture
What evidence supports this thought
Is there another explanation
What is more realistic
Am I trying to predict the future
Am I exaggerating how bad the outcome would be
If a friend had this thought, what would I say to them

The goal is not blind positivity. It is to create a more balanced and helpful thought.

Alternative Behaviors
The workbook also encourages asking what action would be more helpful in the moment. This includes choosing a coping behavior that supports the situation rather than feeding fear or hopelessness.

Helpful options mentioned in the workbook include deep breathing, distraction, seeking support, doing a pleasant activity, using an alternative response worksheet, or taking one small action toward the desired outcome.

Alternative Feelings
Changing thoughts and behaviors can change feelings over time. The workbook asks what a person might feel if they responded differently, and how new thoughts or actions might create more helpful emotions such as calm, reassurance, hope, excitement, or contentment.

It also suggests reminding yourself that strong feelings are normal body reactions and that they will pass.

Self-Affirmations
The workbook includes self-affirmations as a way to balance self-criticism. Effective affirmations are short, personal, positive, and focused on the self, often beginning with “I am.”

Examples follow themes such as being hardworking, kind, proud, thankful, strong, or capable. The workbook suggests using compliments you believe, qualities a loved one would notice, or moments you felt proud of yourself.

Thought Tips for Overthinking
Several thought-management tools are included for people who get stuck in loops. These include interrupting a thought, redirecting attention to something engaging, labeling thoughts with phrases like “I am thinking…” or “My mind is having the thought that…,” and using imagery such as clouds in the sky or leaves on a stream to let thoughts pass.

The workbook also recommends gratitude practice and trying to understand where repetitive thoughts come from instead of only fighting with them.

Guided Imagery
Guided imagery is another relaxation strategy. It involves imagining a restful place as vividly as possible using sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch.

The workbook suggests slowly exploring this place in the mind and allowing worries to drift away while focusing on sensory details. It notes that zoning out, yawning, heaviness, or small muscle twitches can be normal during imagery practice.

Helpful Depression Behaviors
The workbook offers additional behavior ideas for improving mood. These include getting a little sunlight, giving yourself a massage with a tennis ball, practicing yoga, standing up straight, smiling, laughing, using progressive muscle relaxation, listening to or creating music, dancing, and journaling.

The workbook frames these as small actions that can gently shift mood, body state, and thinking.

Progressive Muscle Relaxation
Progressive muscle relaxation is included as a longer body-based calming method. It involves tensing and then releasing different muscle groups so you can feel the difference between tension and relaxation.

The script moves through fists, arms, forehead, eyes, head and neck, back, shoulders, chest, thighs, calves, feet, and then the whole body. It encourages slow breathing and noticing the contrast between tightness and release.

Identifying Triggers
Triggers can be external or internal. External triggers include places, social settings, activities, or pressure to perform. Internal triggers include emotions, physical sensations, thoughts, or mental images.

The workbook notes that sometimes the symptoms themselves become triggers, such as noticing a racing heart and then becoming more anxious because of it. Identifying triggers helps you know when to increase coping practice.

Building a Personal Plan
Toward the end, the workbook encourages putting everything together into a personal plan. This includes naming your main emotional, physical, cognitive, and behavioral symptoms, your common triggers, the thinking styles that catch you, the relaxation tools that help most, and the best time and place to practice them.

It also encourages setting specific, achievable, measurable goals related to anxiety, depression, and self-care over the coming months.

Key Support Use Cases
Help a user calm down in the moment with square breathing, breath counting, grounding, or guided imagery

Help a user rebuild routine through sleep hygiene, exercise, self-care, pleasant activities, and small mastery tasks

Help a user understand how thoughts, feelings, body sensations, and behaviors interact in anxiety or depression

Safety Notes
The workbook notes that depression can include suicidal thoughts or preoccupation with death, which should be taken seriously and supported with additional help

If symptoms feel overwhelming, persistent, or difficult to manage alone, extra support from a mental health professional or other trusted support may be needed
