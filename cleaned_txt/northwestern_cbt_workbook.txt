About This Workbook
This workbook presents cognitive behavioral therapy as a practical way to improve mood by changing thoughts and behaviors in stressful situations. It focuses on the interaction between situations, thoughts, emotions, and behaviors, and teaches skills for interrupting negative spirals.

The material is structured around everyday coping rather than theory alone. It includes mood tracking, behavior planning, identifying automatic thoughts, challenging unhelpful thinking, building support, managing worry, relaxation, and maintaining gains over time.

Core CBT Model
CBT is based on the idea that situations trigger thoughts, emotions, and behaviors that influence one another. Some reactions help people cope, while others deepen stress, anxiety, or depression.

A simple CBT check-in includes:
Situation: what happened
Thoughts: what went through your mind
Emotions: what you felt
Behaviors: what you did next

This model can be used with negative or positive experiences. It is especially useful when a person feels stuck, overwhelmed, guilty, hopeless, avoidant, or self-critical.

How Negative Spirals Form
The workbook describes a common depressive tailspin: feel down, do less, feel worse, do even less. Reduced activity often lowers mood further, which then makes action feel even harder.

CBT aims to reverse this cycle by helping people do more helpful actions, question unhelpful thoughts, and notice that even small changes can improve mood.

Goals for Change
When life feels overwhelming, therapy works better if broad problems are translated into specific goals. The workbook recommends identifying the most important current problems and then turning them into manageable goals.

Useful goals are:
Time-limited
Specific
Under your control
Positive in wording
Measurable

A strong goal focuses on a concrete behavior rather than a vague wish. For example, “I will spend 20 minutes each day doing a self-nurturing activity” is more workable than “I should feel better.”

Breaking Problems Into Steps
The workbook recommends looking closely at a problem before trying to solve it. Helpful questions include when the problem happens, what seems to trigger it, what has been tried before, and how distressing it feels right now.

This makes it easier to break a large struggle into smaller actions. It also helps separate the problem itself from harsh labels like lazy, weak, or incapable.

Progress Is Gradual
The workbook emphasizes that progress is rarely smooth. Change often looks like “two steps forward and one step back,” with setbacks mixed into real improvement.

A setback does not erase progress. It means stress is high or old habits have reappeared, and the next step is to return to the skills rather than give up on them.

Mood Tracking
Mood tracking helps people notice changes that might otherwise be missed. The workbook uses a 1 to 10 mood scale, from very depressed to very happy, to monitor how mood shifts across the day or week.

A practical mood log can include:
Morning mood
Midday mood
Evening mood
Daily average mood
Two main reasons for each rating

Tracking mood alongside events can reveal patterns, such as specific situations that improve mood or predict a downturn.

Pleasant Activities
The workbook explains that pleasant activities can reduce depression and increase a sense of control. When mood drops, activity often drops too, and enjoyable experiences become less frequent, which can worsen mood further.

Adding pleasant events back into daily life can help reverse that cycle. The workbook encourages identifying activities that are realistic, meaningful, and easy enough to begin soon.

Choosing Pleasant Activities
A useful pleasant-activities list includes things that are enjoyable, interesting, calming, social, creative, physical, or personally meaningful. The workbook provides examples such as walking, gardening, reading, listening to music, talking with a friend, doing hobbies, helping others, spiritual activities, and simple outings.

When choosing activities, it helps to rate both how pleasant the activity feels and how often it has been done recently. This can highlight what has quietly disappeared from daily life.

Scheduling Activities
The workbook recommends starting with one or two activities rather than planning too much at once. Pleasant activities should be scheduled with the same seriousness as other responsibilities.

Even if interest is low at first, doing the activity can still help. Early on, a person may feel like they are just going through the motions, but repeated action can gradually improve mood and make enjoyment easier to access again.

Daily Mood and Activity Link
The workbook recommends tracking both pleasant activities and mood for about a week. This helps test whether activity changes are actually helping.

A simple log can include:
The day
Pleasant activity or activities completed
Average mood that day

This kind of tracking can help a person see which activities reliably support mood and which days need more structure or support.

Automatic Thoughts
Automatic thoughts are the quick thoughts that appear in response to situations. They may happen so fast that a person notices the emotion before noticing the thought.

Helpful automatic thoughts can support coping, while unhelpful automatic thoughts can create anxiety, guilt, anger, hopelessness, or depression. These thoughts can be about the situation, the self, other people, or the future.

Identifying Unhelpful Thoughts
The workbook teaches awareness as the first step in changing thinking. When a strong emotion shows up, it helps to slow down and ask what thought just passed through the mind.

A reusable thought-monitoring format includes:
Situation: what happened, where, when, and with whom
Emotions: what you felt and how strong each feeling was from 1 to 10
Automatic thoughts: the specific thoughts that came up
Belief rating: how strongly you believed each thought from 1 to 10

This structure is called an Unhelpful Thoughts Diary and is meant to train awareness before trying to change anything.

Questions to Find Thoughts
The workbook suggests several prompts for identifying automatic thoughts. These prompts are especially useful when a person knows they feel bad but cannot tell what they were thinking.

Helpful prompts include:
What was going through my mind right then
What does this say about me, my life, or my future
What am I afraid might happen
What is the worst thing that could happen if this were true
What does this mean about how others see me
What images or memories came up

Common Thought Patterns
The workbook includes several frequent unhelpful thinking styles. Recognizing the pattern can make it easier to challenge the thought instead of automatically accepting it.

Common patterns include:
Should thinking, where rigid rules create guilt or anxiety
Black-and-white thinking, where only extremes seem possible
Catastrophizing, where the future is assumed to be disastrous
Minimization, where positive events are downplayed
Disqualifying the positive, where praise or success is dismissed
Mind reading, where others’ thoughts are assumed
Overgeneralization, where one event becomes a sweeping conclusion
Emotional reasoning, where feelings are treated as facts
Name calling or blaming, where harsh labels are used
“If only” thinking, where regret about the past blocks the present

Evaluating Thoughts
After identifying an unhelpful thought, the next step is to examine how realistic it is. The workbook emphasizes that a thought can feel true without being fully accurate.

Helpful questions include:
What is the evidence for and against this thought
Am I jumping to conclusions
Am I thinking only in extremes
Are there in-between possibilities
Am I ignoring important positives
Am I blaming myself for something outside my control

Creating Balanced Thoughts
Balanced thoughts do not require fake positivity. They aim to be more realistic, fair, and useful than the original automatic thought.

A balanced thought often:
Acknowledges the difficulty
Includes missing evidence
Makes room for uncertainty
Recognizes coping ability
Uses kinder language

For example, a thought like “They ignored me because I am a burden” can be reframed into something more balanced such as “They may have been distracted, and there is evidence they still care about me.”

Questions for Reframing
The workbook offers extra questions to help generate alternative thoughts. These can be used when the mind feels stuck in one interpretation.

Useful reframing questions include:
What makes me think this thought is true
What is the worst that could happen, and what is the best
How might I see this when I am not feeling down
If someone I care about had this thought, what would I say to them
Will I see this differently five years from now
What strengths or positives am I overlooking

Changing Self-Talk
The workbook notes that language matters. Harsh labels such as “I’m stupid” or “I’m a loser” can intensify emotional pain and keep a negative spiral going.

A more helpful approach is compassionate, accurate language, such as “I made a mistake, but I can learn from it.” This does not deny problems; it reduces unnecessary self-attack.

Act-As-If Skill
One strategy in the workbook is to think about how a respected, caring person might speak in the same situation. Then try talking to yourself “as if” you were using that wiser, kinder voice.

This may feel unnatural at first. With repetition, it can become a practical way to interrupt harsh inner dialogue and build a more supportive internal tone.

Credit the Positives
When stressed or depressed, people often focus almost entirely on negatives. The workbook encourages deliberately noticing positive events, strengths, or moments of effort so they are not filtered out.

This can include giving yourself credit for small actions, recognizing encouragement from others, or identifying positive effects of recent choices.

Positive Affirmations
The workbook also suggests using short personal statements during periods of overwhelm. These are not meant to be empty slogans but reminders of worth, strength, or possibility.

Examples are brief self-supportive statements such as being a good person, being capable of love, or being able to grow and cope.

Examining Consequences
A helpful way to loosen an unhelpful belief is to examine its consequences. A thought may seem protective, but if it increases isolation, shame, anxiety, avoidance, or hopelessness, it may not be serving you well.

Looking at emotional, physical, and social consequences can increase motivation to challenge that thought.

Scale Technique
The workbook includes a simple weighing exercise for stubborn thoughts, emotions, or behavior patterns. The idea is to list the advantages and disadvantages of continuing the pattern.

This can be useful when a behavior feels both protective and costly, such as over-responsibility, constant worry, or avoiding self-care because guilt feels stronger than personal needs.

Worry and Overthinking
The workbook includes a module on anxiety and worry. It notes that worry can signal that something needs attention, but the act of worrying itself is not the same as solving the problem.

A practical check is to ask:
Is this a real problem I can act on
How likely is the feared outcome
Would the consequences truly be as catastrophic as they feel
Is worrying helping me right now

Estimating Risk More Realistically
The anxiety section encourages comparing emotional certainty with realistic likelihood. A feared outcome may feel almost certain emotionally, yet be much less likely when examined carefully.

It also recommends writing alternative possibilities and coping statements, such as reminders of past competence, evidence of support, or the fact that setbacks are survivable.

Thinking Through the Worst Case
One worry strategy is to imagine the feared worst-case scenario in a deliberate way instead of bouncing between fear and reassurance. The goal is to think it through enough to identify how you would cope if it actually happened.

This can reduce fear because the mind stops treating the outcome as an undefined disaster. Once coping options become visible, the situation often feels less overwhelming.

Scheduled Worry Time
The workbook includes “worry time” as a containment strategy. Instead of worrying all day, a person writes worries down and postpones them to a set time later.

A simple version is:
Write the worry on a list when it shows up
Return attention to the present task
Set aside a short, limited period later to review the worries
End worry time on schedule and shift to another activity

This can help reduce endless worry loops and protect the rest of the day.

Anxiety Thought Balancing
The anxiety diary in the workbook adds two useful columns: actual danger and available resources. This helps balance fear with coping ability.

A practical anxiety thought record can include:
Situation
Anxious emotions
Automatic worry thoughts
Balanced thoughts
Balanced risk: is it truly dangerous
Resources: what strengths, supports, and coping tools are available

Relaxation and Stress Response
The relaxation section explains stress in body terms. The sympathetic nervous system increases arousal, while the parasympathetic nervous system helps cool the body back down.

Relaxation skills can therefore be used not only for anxiety, but also for depression, sleep problems, headaches, and general stress overload.

Social Support
The workbook also highlights social support as a major mood resource. Support can include emotional comfort, practical help, companionship, belonging, and assistance with tasks.

When people feel supported, they often cope better under stress. The workbook encourages identifying where support is strong, where it is limited, and what obstacles interfere with asking for or receiving help.

Self-Monitoring and Review
A recurring theme is that awareness creates options. Tracking mood, situations, thoughts, and actions makes it easier to notice patterns and choose a skill intentionally rather than reacting automatically.

The workbook repeatedly recommends short reviews of what was learned, what worked, what got in the way, and what small action to practice next.

Maintenance and Relapse Prevention
The final section focuses on keeping gains after symptoms improve. It recommends identifying personal warning signs in thoughts, behaviors, emotions, and physical symptoms so action can be taken early.

A practical maintenance plan includes:
List your early warning signs
List your most common unhelpful thoughts
Write down responses that help challenge those thoughts
Identify strategies that help with daily hassles
Create prompts to remember those strategies
Plan ahead for future challenging situations
List people who can help when support is needed

Compact Support Templates
The workbook can be translated into a few reusable support prompts:
Situation: What happened
Thought: What went through my mind
Feeling: What did I feel, and how strong was it
Behavior: What did I do next
Alternative thought: What is a more balanced view
Next step: What small helpful action can I take now

A second practical prompt for low mood is:
What small pleasant or meaningful activity can I do today
When will I do it
How likely am I to follow through
What could get in the way
How will I handle that

Key Support Use Cases
Help a user map a difficult moment into situation, thought, feeling, and behavior

Help a user identify automatic thoughts and common distortions such as catastrophizing, should thinking, or mind reading

Help a user generate balanced thoughts and choose one small next action

Help a user rebuild mood through pleasant activities, scheduling, and progress tracking

Help a user manage worry with realistic risk estimates, coping statements, worst-case planning, and scheduled worry time

Safety Notes
The workbook is built for mood problems and depressive spirals, so persistent worsening mood, severe hopelessness, or inability to function may require additional support beyond self-help tools

If warning signs are increasing, it is important to intervene early with coping skills, social support, and additional mental health support when needed
