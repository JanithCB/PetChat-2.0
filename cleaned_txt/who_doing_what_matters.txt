About This Guide
This is a practical stress-management guide for coping with adversity. It is designed for people experiencing stress from personal difficulties, work problems, uncertainty, conflict, illness, danger, displacement, or other overwhelming situations. Stress can affect anyone, and this guide offers simple skills that can be practised for a few minutes at a time.

If the exercises in this guide are not enough to help with intolerable stress, seek support from relevant health or social services or from trusted people in your community.

Understanding Stress
Stress means feeling troubled or threatened by life. It can arise from major threats or from smaller ongoing problems.

High stress often affects both body and mind. Common effects include headaches, pain, tight muscles, upset stomach, sleep problems, difficulty focusing, anger, guilt, sadness, worry, tiredness, appetite changes, and repeated thoughts about the past or feared future.

Difficult thoughts and feelings are a natural part of stress. The problem is not that they appear, but that people can get pulled away by them and lose contact with what matters.

Getting Hooked
When difficult thoughts and feelings take over attention, a person can become “hooked.” This can happen suddenly, even in the middle of daily life, relationships, or routine activities.

When people are hooked, they may move away from what matters to them. They may argue more, withdraw from loved ones, give up, isolate themselves, or stay in bed for long periods.

The guide describes these patterns as moving away from values. Values are the kind of person someone wants to be, such as caring, calm, brave, responsible, attentive, or kind.

Engaging With the Present
One way to handle stress is to practise engagement. To engage means to give full attention to what you are doing right now.

This can be practised during ordinary activities such as drinking water, tea, or coffee; talking to family or friends; playing with children; washing; cooking; or doing chores.

For example, while drinking, notice the smell, colour, taste, temperature, and movement of swallowing. If thoughts pull you away, gently refocus on the drink and continue.

Engaging helps bring attention back to the present moment. It can make everyday activities feel steadier and sometimes more satisfying, even when life is stressful.

Grounding During Emotional Storms
Sometimes thoughts and feelings become so intense that they feel like an emotional storm. In those moments, the goal is not to make the storm disappear, but to steady yourself until it passes.

Grounding begins by noticing what you are feeling and thinking. Then slow down and connect with your body.

A simple grounding sequence:
Notice what you are feeling and thinking.
Slow your breathing.
Breathe out gently.
Let your lungs refill slowly.
Press your feet into the floor.
Stretch your arms or press your hands together.

Then refocus on the world around you:
Notice where you are.
Name five things you can see.
Notice three or four things you can hear.
Notice what you can smell.
Touch your knees, the chair, the floor, or another nearby object and notice its texture.

Grounding helps keep you safe and oriented during intense emotion. It does not remove pain, anger, fear, or sadness, but it helps you stay connected to the present and to your ability to act.

Grounding With Breathing
A longer grounding practice uses slow, gentle breathing. First notice thoughts and feelings. Then breathe out slowly and gently, stretch, and press your feet into the ground.

Continue by emptying your lungs slowly and gently, then breathing in slowly and gently. If you start to feel dizzy, light-headed, panicky, anxious, short of breath, or tight-chested, slow your breathing further and keep it shallow rather than deep. Deep breathing too hard or too fast can worsen dizziness.

You can place attention on the breath with curiosity. Notice air moving in and out of the nostrils, the movement of the shoulders, chest, and belly, and keep returning attention to the breath each time the mind wanders.

Short practices may last one or two minutes, and longer practices may last five to ten minutes. To finish, ground yourself again by slowing down, stretching, pressing your feet into the floor, and reconnecting with what you can see, hear, smell, taste, and touch.

Unhooking From Thoughts and Feelings
Trying to get rid of difficult thoughts and feelings often works only briefly, and many common strategies can make life worse. Examples include yelling, avoiding people or situations, staying in bed, isolating, giving up, using substances, starting arguments, or harsh self-criticism.

Instead of struggling with thoughts and feelings, this guide teaches unhooking. Unhooking means noticing that a thought or feeling has taken hold, naming it, and then returning attention to what you are doing.

The three basic steps are:

Notice the difficult thought or feeling.

Name it silently.

Refocus on what you are doing.

Naming can sound like:
Here is tightness in my chest.
Here is anger.
Here is a painful memory.
Here is a thought about the future.
I notice here is sadness.
I notice I have been hooked by fear.

After noticing and naming, bring your attention back to the person or activity in front of you. This could mean returning fully to cooking, eating, walking, washing, or listening to someone.

This skill is meant to be practised repeatedly. Each time you notice you have been pulled away, notice, name, and refocus again.

Practising Unhooking in Daily Life
It is natural for difficult thoughts and feelings to appear under stress. The skill is not to stop them from arising, but to notice them sooner and return to the present more gently.

It can help to set regular times for practice, such as before meals, after meals, while lying awake in bed, or during brief pauses in the day. Practice may not make you sleep, but it can help you feel more rested and less entangled in distress.

Unhelpful thoughts about practice may also show up, such as “this is useless,” “it will not help,” or “I cannot do it.” These thoughts can also be noticed, named, and released in the same way.

Acting on Values
Values are not goals. Goals are things you want to achieve, while values are the way you want to be with yourself, other people, and the world.

A goal may become impossible because of circumstances, but values can still guide action. Even in hard situations, a person can often still act with kindness, care, courage, patience, responsibility, fairness, or persistence.

The guide encourages asking: Do my actions support my values? This helps shift attention from what cannot be controlled to the actions that are still possible right now.

Small Value-Based Actions
Values become real through actions. Actions include what you do, what you say, and how you treat people around you.

Choose a person or relationship that matters to you. Identify the values you want to live by in that relationship, then choose one small realistic action for the next week.

Examples from the guide include playing with a child for ten minutes a day, reading to them, greeting a relative warmly, or expressing gratitude. Even very small actions matter.

If a plan feels too hard, choose something smaller and easier. Small realistic actions are more helpful than large plans that cannot be done.

When Pain Cannot Be Removed
The guide describes three broad responses to a difficult situation:
Leave, if that is possible and better.
Change what can be changed, accept the pain that cannot be changed, and live by your values.
Give up and move away from your values.

When leaving is not possible, the guide emphasizes the second path: take whatever action you can, accept the pain that cannot be changed, and continue living by your values.

This approach combines practical action with acceptance and does not require waiting for all pain to disappear before living meaningfully.

Being Kind to Others
Kindness matters in difficult times. People are often better able to cope when someone is beside them with kindness, care, and understanding.

Even small kind actions can help, including kind words, practical support, and simple human warmth. Examples include saying, “This is really painful,” or “I am here for you.”

The guide encourages noticing suffering in others and responding with small caring actions whenever possible.

Being Kind to Yourself
People under stress often get hooked by harsh self-judgments such as “I am weak,” “I am bad,” “It is my fault,” or “I can never forgive myself.”

These thoughts may be common, but getting entangled in them pulls a person away from their values. The guide recommends noticing and naming such thoughts as unkind thoughts or harsh self-judgments, then grounding and returning attention to the present.

Self-kindness is presented as important, not selfish. If you are kind to yourself, you may have more energy and motivation to help others too.

A simple self-kindness practice is to speak to yourself in a gentler way, for example: This is hard. This hurts. I can still be caring to myself and others.

Making Room for Pain
Another skill in the guide is making room for difficult thoughts and feelings instead of fighting with them. The guide compares thoughts and feelings to weather that moves through the sky. The weather changes, and the sky has room for it.

This practice starts with noticing and naming the thought or feeling. Then observe it with curiosity rather than trying to push it away.

As you breathe out slowly, pause briefly, then breathe in slowly and imagine the breath flowing into and around the pain. The aim is to open up and make room for the experience so it can be there without controlling all of your attention.

A painful feeling can be imagined as an object with a size, shape, colour, and temperature. A difficult thought can be imagined as words or pictures on the pages of a book.

You can imagine the thought resting on your lap or tucked under your arm while you continue to engage with the world around you. The thought may still be present, but it does not have to stop you from living.

Making Room in Practice
To make room:
Notice the difficult thought or feeling.
Name it.
Observe it with curiosity.
Breathe slowly into and around it.
Allow it to be there without fighting it.
Then return attention to the world around you.

When you get pulled out of the exercise, notice that, name it, and return again. The goal is not to make the feeling shrink on command. It may get bigger, smaller, stay the same, or pass. The practice is to allow it and make space for it.

This skill can be used during the day and can also be combined with breathing when lying awake in bed.

Kindness With Touch
The guide includes a gentle exercise for self-kindness and making room. Place a hand, or two hands, gently on a part of the body where you feel pain, such as the heart, belly, or forehead.

Imagine filling the hand with kindness and letting warmth flow into the body. If helpful, imagine breathing through the hand toward the painful feeling.

This can be especially useful when alone or in bed and may be combined with breathing and making room practices.

Practice Reminders
These skills work best with repetition. They are described as learnable skills that become easier with practice.

Grounding can be practised during emotional storms or during ordinary moments. Unhooking can be used any time thoughts and feelings pull attention away. Acting on values can guide daily choices. Being kind and making room can soften the struggle with pain.

Even tiny steps matter. Short, repeated practice can help build steadiness over time.

Key Support Use Cases
Help a user who feels overwhelmed return to the present with grounding steps

Help a user notice intrusive thoughts or emotions without getting pulled away by them

Help a user choose one small value-based action when life feels painful or stuck

Safety Notes
If the exercises are not enough for intolerable stress, seek help from health or social services or from trusted people in the community
